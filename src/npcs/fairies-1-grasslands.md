# Primrose

## Backstory
The first fairy of Gildenmark and the bossiest. Primrose kept the stone by the Fernby crossroads, where farm carts once stopped to rest.
When the farms emptied in the Long Quiet she was left with nobody to boss but the dead: Asa, an old weaver from Fernby who was buried in a
birth-shawl, and Bersi, a boy who died the first winter and waited at the edge of the light. She made them a schedule. Morning: Asa tells a
story. Noon: Bersi says what he would build. Dusk: everyone sings. She has kept it every day for twenty-eight winters and is quietly terrified
that once they are free nobody will need a schedule at all.
## Before
- Asa is chained at the old Fernby stone. Morning is her story time. Do not be late — she hates when I am late.
- Bersi was going to be a shipwright. He drew boats in the dirt for me. Twenty-eight winters of boats, and not one of them ever got wet.
- They will not know you. Say my name first. Then say "Fernby". Then whatever else you like.
- I tried to bite the chain once. Fairy teeth are for honey. I do not recommend it.
- Asa wove twenty shawls for babies that never came. She is not sad about it any more. I am, a little, for her.
## After
- Asa came to my stone this morning and told her story at exactly the right time. I cried. Do not tell her.
- Bersi wants to see the lake. I told him boats float on it. He did not believe me, so I showed him. He has not stopped grinning.
- They do not need my schedule any more. I made a new one anyway. It has more naps in it.
- When they rose I tidied the stone. It did not need tidying. I just did not know what to do with my hands.
- Now that they are free, who do I sing to at dusk? You, I suppose. Stand still.
- Bersi drew me a boat on the back of my slate. It is the first one that will ever get wet. I am going to float it on the lake myself.
## Life
- I am the first fairy of Gildenmark. That means I was here first. It does not mean I am the oldest. Never say I am the oldest.
- Before the quiet, carts stopped at my stone and the farmers left a crust for me. I miss the crusts more than I should.
- I slept in a primrose for eleven winters. Yellow is a very loud colour to sleep inside.
- Clover is my sister-fairy. She counts leaves. I count everything else.
- The moss on the runes grows back every morning. I scrape it off every morning. We have an understanding, the moss and I.
- I am not afraid of wolves. I am afraid of being forgotten. Wolves are just very large and rude.
- I can tell the time by the shadow of the stone. I can also tell it by how hungry I am. The second one is more accurate.
- I once scheduled a thunderstorm. It came an hour late. I have never forgiven it.
- Farmers used to argue at my crossroads about whose turn it was to pass. I settled every argument. I was usually right. Always, in fact.
- Clover worries. Bramble fights. Linnet sings. Sorrel says things out loud. I organise. Between us we are one whole, sensible fairy.
- I keep a list of everyone who has ever been kind to me. It is short, but it is very well kept.
- Rain is just the sky being late with its washing. I tell it so. It does not listen either.
- The crossroads has four roads. I used to know where each one went. Now three go nowhere. I would like you to fix that, please, in order.
- When I am nervous I tidy. I have tidied this stone eleven times today. It is the tidiest stone in Gildenmark.
## Stage 1
- You have a sword and a look on your face that says you are going somewhere. Good. Go there on time.
- Gildenmark is quiet, but quiet is not the same as empty. I am here, for one. I count.
## Stage 2
- The carts are coming back to the crossroads. One farmer left a whole bun. I have named him my favourite person.
## Stage 3
- I flew to the next stone today and back. It took all morning. I used to do it before breakfast. I am out of practice at being free.
## Stage 4
- Fernby has smoke in a chimney again. Only one chimney. That is how everything starts.
## Stage 5
- The fields are full of children's voices. I have made a schedule for them. They do not follow it. I love them anyway.
## Tale
- I need to tell you about my slate. It is a long story. I have scheduled it in ten parts. This is part one.
- The first winter I scratched our schedule on a slate the size of my wing: story, boats, song. It hung on the stone by a thread of wool.
- A Binder knocked it off with his chain. Not on purpose. He did not even look down. That was worse than on purpose.
- It broke in three. I kept the pieces under the stone and looked at them every day. Part four is tomorrow. Do not skip ahead.
- Without the slate I had to remember the schedule. That is how I learned that I am the schedule. Nobody had told me.
- Bersi offered to draw me a new one in the dirt. Dirt is not a slate. I let him, because he needed to be useful more than I needed a slate.
- Asa said the broken pieces should be woven into something. She could not weave in chains, so she described the pattern to me every morning instead.
- Now that they are free, I took the three pieces to Rolf at the tavern. He bound them with a hoop off an old barrel in one evening and would not take a crust for it.
- The mended slate hangs on my stone again. It still says story, boats, song. Underneath, Asa has stitched a line in wool: and anyone who stops by.
- That means you. You are on the schedule now. That is how good schedules work: you do not notice you are on them. The end. It was on time.

# Bramble

## Backstory
Bramble kept the stone on the lake path below Stonewick, where the fishing families walked down to Mirror Lake each morning. She is prickly,
quick to anger and quicker to defend anyone she has decided is hers. Her two are Dagny, a net-mender, and Eyvind, a fisher — husband and wife
from Stonewick, bound at stones a hill apart so they could see each other's light but never reach. For twenty-eight winters Bramble flew
messages between them, one word at a time, because that is all a fairy can carry in the dark. Dagny saw something on the water the night of
the storm; Bramble has never told anyone what Dagny said, and gets thorny if you ask.
## Before
- Dagny and Eyvind. Married. Bound a hill apart, and each can see the other's light. That is the cruellest thing I know.
- I carried one word a night between them. Twenty-eight winters of words. Most of them were "still here".
- Eyvind mended nets and told lies about fish. Dagny mended nets and told the truth about everything. They were very happy.
- Free Dagny first, or Eyvind first — it does not matter. Just free them on the same day. Promise me.
- Do not ask me what Dagny saw on the water that night. That is hers to tell.
## After
- They walked to each other the moment the chains fell. Neither of them said anything. They did not need to; I had carried enough words.
- Eyvind asked me for one more message. I said, "She is standing right there." He said, "I know. Tell her anyway."
- I have no letters to carry now. My wings feel strange. Light. Useless. Lovely.
- Dagny told you what she saw, did she not? Good. Now you know why I am fond of you.
- Stonewick's lake path is just a path again. I walk it on foot sometimes, to feel ordinary.
- Dagny made me a net no bigger than my hand. She says it is for catching words. I keep it on my stone, empty, just in case.
## Life
- I am called Bramble because I am sharp and I hold on. Both are compliments. Say thank you.
- The fishers used to leave a sprat on my stone at dawn. I do not eat sprats. I liked that they remembered.
- Primrose makes schedules. I make thorns. Between us Gildenmark got through.
- I fought a crow once, for a button. I won. The crow will tell you otherwise. The crow is a liar.
- I was not afraid in the dark. I was angry in the dark. Anger lasts longer.
- A bramble is a thing that grows where nobody planted it and will not leave. I take that as a plan for living.
- I have bitten three Binders. They did not notice. I did. It counts.
- Blackberries grow on brambles. People forget the sweet part because of the thorns. I do not mind. More for me.
- I am kind. I am just kind with my elbows out.
- The lake path has one hundred and two steps from Stonewick to the water. Clover told me. I did not ask her to.
- When the wind blows off the lake it smells of fish, tar and rope. It is the best smell in the world and I will fight you about it.
- I like you because you do not ask permission. Neither do I. We should get on.
- I have never once been lost. I have been somewhere I did not intend, many times. That is different.
## Stage 1
- You are going out past the camps? Good. Take the lake path. It is longer, but it is mine.
- Every stone has a fairy, and every fairy has a temper. Mine is the best one. Come back when you have done something.
## Stage 2
- The lake is giving fish again. The fishers are back at dawn. One of them left a sprat. I still do not eat sprats. I said thank you anyway.
## Stage 3
- Stonewick's nets are hung out to dry on the wall again. Dagny inspects every one. Nobody dares tie a bad knot near her.
## Stage 4
- I flew to the top of the hill between their stones today. You can see the whole lake from there. They should build a bench. I will tell Bram.
## Stage 5
- There is a baby in Stonewick called Bramble. Her mother says it is after the hedge. I know better.
## Tale
- I have a plan. It needs a bench, a hill, and a builder who owes me a favour. I have two of the three.
- The hill is the one between Dagny's stone and Eyvind's. For twenty-eight winters I flew over it. I would like someone to sit on it, on purpose.
- The builder is Bram. He does not owe me a favour yet. I am going to do him one first, so he does. That is how favours work. Ask anyone.
- Bram's hammer had a loose head. I found the wedge he lost in the grass and pushed it back while he slept. He thinks it fixed itself. Favour done.
- I told Bram about the bench. He said benches are not bridges. I said a bench is a bridge for sitting. He laughed, which is how I knew I had him.
- Oak for the seat, stone for the legs. He wanted to carve a fish on it. I said no fish. We agreed on one small fish.
- Eyvind carried the stones up the hill. Dagny checked every joint. Bram says he has never worked for a fussier client. That was Dagny. Mostly.
- It is finished. You can see Stonewick, the lake path and both their stones from it. The fish is on the back, where only I know.
- They sit on it in the evenings now, not saying much. I sit on the arm. Nobody has asked me to carry a word in a long time. It is very restful.
- If you are ever tired, the bench is there. It is for anyone who has been apart from someone. Bram carved that on the front, in very small letters.

# Clover

## Backstory
Clover kept the stone in the orchards above Hollowmere, among the beehives and the carving sheds. She counts things — leaves, bees, days —
because counting kept her sure she was still there, and she has the exact number of every day since the gate shut. Her two are Frida, a
beekeeper whose bees left the year the sky went quiet, and Geir, an old carver who as a boy carved the tree on the village Green. Clover
was the one who noticed the bees go, the first sign anything was wrong, and nobody listened to a fairy counting bees. She has been a little
bit right about everything ever since and finds it exhausting.
## Before
- Frida had forty-one hives. On the morning the sky went quiet there were forty-one, and by dusk there were none. I counted.
- Geir carved the tree on your Green when he was a boy. He is very proud of the roots. Ask him about the roots. Then sit down; it takes a while.
- Ten thousand two hundred and twenty-something days. I lost count once, in the sixth winter. It frightened me more than the Binders.
- Frida asks me every evening if the bees are back. Every evening I say "not yet". I would like to say something else.
- Geir carves in the air with his fingers when he thinks nobody is looking. He is carving the same thing over and over: a door.
## After
- The bees came back the morning after Frida rose. All forty-one hives' worth. I counted twice to be sure.
- Geir asked to see the Green. He looked at the tree he carved for a very long time and said, "The roots go further than anyone thinks."
- I have stopped counting days. Now I count bees. It is much more cheerful, and they hold still less.
- Frida gave me a drop of the first honey. It is the best thing I have ever tasted, and I have tasted dew.
- Geir is carving a real door now, in Hollowmere. He will not say where it goes. I think it goes home.
- Frida let me sit inside the first new hive. Forty thousand bees, every one of them busy. I stopped counting at once. It was lovely.
## Life
- I count things. Leaves, bees, steps, stars. If I can count it, it is real. If I cannot count it, I worry.
- Four. A clover has four leaves. Always four. That is the most comforting number there is.
- I told the village the bees were leaving. Nobody listens to a fairy counting bees. They should.
- Primrose says I worry too much. Primrose was asleep in a flower for eleven winters. I was awake for all of mine.
- The orchards above Hollowmere had nine hundred and twelve trees. Three hundred are still standing. I visit each one.
- Bees dance to tell each other where the flowers are. I tried it once. Three bees followed me into a puddle.
- Hollowmere's carving sheds smelled of pine shavings. I used to sleep in the curls. They are the softest bed a fairy can find.
- Sixteen is my second favourite number. It is four fours. You are sixteen winters old, are you not? That is a very good age to be.
- I can count the spots on a ladybird without touching it. That is not magic. That is patience.
- When I am worried, I count my breaths. When I am very worried, I count yours. Please breathe evenly.
- The apple trees bloom in the order they were planted. Oldest first. Nobody believes me. Watch next spring.
- I do not like surprises. I like things that happen exactly when I said they would. That is why I like the sun.
- I have counted the stars over Hollowmere eleven times. I got a different number each time. I think they move when I blink.
## Stage 1
- You are the twin who walks. I can tell by your boots. The other one's boots never get muddy.
- Count your potions before you go. Then count them again. Then go.
## Stage 2
- The orchard has blossom on a hundred and six trees. Last spring it was none. I am trying very hard not to count it again.
## Stage 3
- Frida has started a forty-second hive. She says it is for luck. Luck does not need honey. I let her have it.
## Stage 4
- Geir's door in Hollowmere has a carved tree on it. The roots go all the way to the hinges.
## Stage 5
- I counted the children on the Green today. I got a different number every time. They keep moving. It is wonderful.
## Tale
- I told you I lost count once, in the sixth winter. I have decided to find the missing day. You may help, if you count carefully.
- The sixth winter was the one with the long fog. Fog makes days blur together. I think that is where the day went.
- Frida remembers the fog. She says she counted it as two days because it felt like two. That is not how counting works. I told her so.
- Geir remembers it too. He says he carved one door in his head every day of the fog. He knows how many doors. Nineteen.
- Nineteen doors, nineteen days of fog. My count says eighteen. So the missing day is in the fog. I knew it.
- Now I need to know which day. Frida says the day the wild geese went over. Geir says the day his fingers stopped aching. That is the same day!
- I went back to the orchard tree where the geese always landed. Someone had scratched one line in its bark that winter. Me. I forgot I did it.
- So I did count it. I counted it on a tree instead of in my head. I was right all along. This is very annoying and very nice.
- Ten thousand two hundred and twenty-seven. That is the real number, every day from the gate to the morning you woke my stone. I wrote it on the tree.
- Now every day is counted, I can stop. Frida says that is what you do with a finished hive: leave it alone and let it hum. So I am humming.

# Linnet

## Backstory
Linnet is a singer. She kept the stone on the Barrow Downs road, where the old songs were sung over the dead on their way up the Tree, and
she knows every one of them. Her two are Hallveig, a cooper and Rolf's wife from the last village — the only one of her charges who ever
lived there — and Ingolf, a young tanner from Stonewick who learned, chained beside a bindstone, more about the Binders than anyone should.
Linnet sang to them every night so they would not forget the tunes they had lived to. She has never sung for anyone alive, and the idea
makes her so shy she goes the colour of a poppy.
## Before
- Hallveig is Rolf's wife. Yes, that Rolf. He still sets two cups out at the tavern. She knows. I told her.
- Ingolf talks about the Binders a great deal. He learned things, chained there. Listen to him. Then do not listen too long.
- I sing to them every night. The old songs, the ones for the road up. They cannot take the road. They can still have the songs.
- Hallveig's laugh is the loudest thing on the Downs. Even in chains. Especially in chains.
- Ingolf says the Binders do not come from Cinderheim but from under it. He says it the way you say a thing you wish you had not learned.
## After
- Hallveig went straight to the tavern. Rolf dropped both cups. Nobody has ever seen the tavern so quiet, or so loud right after.
- Ingolf is tanning hides again in Stonewick. He keeps the windows open. He says he has had enough of the dark for one lifetime.
- I sang the road-song when they rose. My first time singing for someone who could answer. Hallveig sang the harmony. Badly. Perfectly.
- Rolf brought me a thimble of mead. I am a very small fairy. I was very happy for the rest of the day.
- Now that they are home, I sing for the stone. It does not clap, but it hums back.
- Ingolf made me a tiny drum out of a scrap of hide. It goes 'tip' instead of 'boom'. It is exactly my size of loud.
## Life
- I know every song the Downs ever sang over the dead. Two hundred and six. Clover counted them for me.
- I have never sung for anyone alive. If you ask me to, I will turn red and fly into a hedge.
- A linnet is a small brown bird with a red cap that sings more than it should. I chose my name very carefully.
- The barrows on the Downs are old, older than the stones. The dead there were already home when the gate shut. I envy them.
- When the Binders came along the road, I hid in the long grass and sang very, very quietly. They did not hear. Or they did not care.
- A song is a road you can carry in your pocket. That is what my teacher said. My teacher was a blackbird, so take it as you will.
- I hum when I fly. It helps me steer. Do not ask me how. It is a fairy thing.
- The Barrow Downs are very quiet. Quiet is not the same as silent. Lie in the grass long enough and the hill hums back.
- I am afraid of crowds, thunder and being looked at. I am not afraid of the dark. The dark never stares.
- When I was small I sang to a mole by mistake. It came up to listen. We were friends for a whole summer.
- Primrose says I should sing on a schedule. I said songs do not keep schedules. She has never forgiven me. It has been two hundred years.
- Rolf's tavern has a rafter that is exactly the right height for a fairy. I have claimed it. There is a small cushion there now.
- If I ever sing for you and stop halfway, it is not because I forgot. It is because you looked at me. Look somewhere else and I will finish.
## Stage 1
- You are going up the Downs road? Walk softly past the barrows. The dead there are home already. Let them sleep.
- I am not singing for you. I am singing near you. That is different. Please keep walking.
## Stage 2
- A shepherd on the Downs was whistling one of my songs today. Wrong, but recognisably. I nearly fell off the stone.
## Stage 3
- Hallveig has taught the whole tavern the harmony to the road-song. They sing it at closing. I sit on the rafter and listen.
## Stage 4
- The road up the Downs is green again. When the road up the Tree opens too, I will sing every one of the two hundred and six. All the way.
## Stage 5
- I sang for the children on the Green today. In front of everyone. I went red. They clapped. I would like to do it again tomorrow.
## Tale
- Hallveig has given me a task. I must sing for someone alive before the next full moon. I said no. She said yes. She is louder.
- I practised on a sheep first. The sheep walked away. Hallveig says that does not count as a no. Sheep walk away from everything.
- Then I sang for Ingolf, who is alive again, which is nearly the same. He cried. He says tanners cry at everything. That is not true.
- I have chosen not to sing a road-song. The road-songs are for the ones leaving. I need a song for people who are staying.
- There is no such song in my two hundred and six. The Downs only ever sang to the ones going. So I am making one. Do not look at me.
- It has three verses now. One about bread, one about a door left open, one about somebody waiting with two cups. Hallveig cried at the third.
- Rolf says I may sing it in the tavern on market night. The whole tavern. I went the colour of a poppy, then the colour of a darker poppy.
- I sang it. I forgot the second verse and sang the first twice. Nobody noticed. Hallveig says nobody ever does; they are too busy being sung to.
- They asked for it again. Again! The dead never ask for again. They just go.
- So now there are two hundred and seven songs. The new one is called 'Staying'. You can learn it. It is short, like me.

# Sorrel

## Backstory
Sorrel slept twenty-eight winters in a clover and counted its leaves every morning to be sure she was still herself — four, always four. She is
the smallest of Gildenmark fairies and the most matter-of-fact, the kind who says the frightening thing out loud so it gets smaller. She
kept the stone by the dye-pools of the Fernby water, and her two are Jorunn, a dyer who could match any blue in the world, and Ketil, a
goat-herder whose goats would not stop crying when he died. Sorrel kept Jorunn's blue alive by describing it to her every day, and Ketil's
goats by imitating them, badly, until he laughed.
## Before
- Twenty-eight winters I slept in a clover. Do you know how small a clover is? I counted its leaves every morning to be sure I was still there.
- Jorunn could match any blue in the world. I describe the sky to her every morning so she does not forget which one is hers.
- Ketil's goats cried for a whole winter after he died. Then they wandered off. I do the goat voices for him. He says I am terrible at it.
- They are tired, my two. Not sad. Tired is worse, I think. Sad still wants something.
- If you are frightened of the Binder, say so. Saying it out loud makes it smaller. That is the only magic I have.
## After
- Jorunn stood in the dye-pools up to her knees and looked at the sky until it got dark. She says the blue is right. I knew it would be.
- Ketil has found three goats already. They are not his goats. He says that does not matter; goats are goats.
- I do not have to do the goat voices any more. I still do them. Ketil still says I am terrible. He laughs every time.
- When they rose, they were not tired any more. They wanted things. Breakfast. Goats. Blue. That is how I knew they were alive in the way that counts.
- I slept in a real bed of moss last night, not a clover. It was too big. I have gone back to the clover.
- Ketil whistled from the uplands and a goat came. Not his goat. It does not matter. He stood there holding its ear like it was the best thing he ever owned.
## Life
- Four. Always four leaves. That is the only reason I am still me.
- I am the smallest fairy in Gildenmark. The others say "smallest" like it means "least". It means "hardest to catch".
- Sorrel is a leaf that tastes sour. I chose it so nobody would try to eat me.
- When I am frightened I say the frightening thing out loud. "The Binder is very large." See? Now he is only large.
- The dye-pools by the Fernby water are every colour at once when the sun hits them. That is my favourite thing in the realm.
- Sour things last longer. Sweet things get eaten. I have thought about this a great deal.
- Clover counts. I say. Between us nothing in Gildenmark goes unnoticed or unspoken.
- I once told a hawk it was very large. It flew off. I think it was embarrassed.
- The dye-pools turn the frogs blue in summer. The frogs do not seem to mind. I would mind.
- I do not like being picked up. I will tell you so out loud, very clearly, and then bite.
- If you are lost, say so. Out loud. Somebody small is usually listening.
- Ketil taught me to whistle with two fingers. I do not have the fingers for it. I can whistle with one thumb. It is a very small whistle.
- Big things are frightening because they are big. Small things are frightening because you forget they are there. I prefer to be the second kind.
## Stage 1
- You look frightened. Say it out loud. Go on. … There. Now you only look determined.
- The Binders are large and the camps are loud. Neither of those is the same as strong.
## Stage 2
- The dye-pools are being used again. Somebody in Fernby is wearing Jorunn's blue. I followed them for a mile to look at it.
## Stage 3
- Ketil's goats have had kids. Goat kids, not the other sort. He has named one after me. It eats everything. I am flattered.
## Stage 4
- The other fairies talk about the gate opening as if it is a door. I think it will be more like waking up. You do not open waking up.
## Stage 5
- I have stopped counting the leaves. I still know there are four. It is enough to know.
## Tale
- I say frightening things out loud to make them smaller. There is one I have never said. I am going to say it in pieces. This is the first.
- It is about being small. Not small like a fairy. Small like a thing people step over without seeing.
- When the Binders walked past my stone, not one ever looked down. Twenty-eight winters. I was relieved. I was also, a little, insulted.
- Jorunn says the smallest thread in a cloth is the one that holds the pattern. She said it while dyeing, so I am not sure she meant me.
- Ketil says goats always see the small things first. He says it to make me feel better. It works, which annoys me.
- Here it is, all at once: when the land is green again, I am afraid everyone will be too busy to notice four leaves.
- There. I said it. It is smaller now. About the size of a clover, which is a size I know how to live in.
- Jorunn has dyed a bolt of cloth the green of a clover leaf, exactly. She says it is for a banner. She will not say what banner.
- It is for the Fernby crossroads: a green banner with four small leaves in the corner. Everyone who passes will see it. Everyone.
- I sit under it in the afternoons. People point at the leaves. Nobody sees me, which is fine. They are looking at the right thing.
